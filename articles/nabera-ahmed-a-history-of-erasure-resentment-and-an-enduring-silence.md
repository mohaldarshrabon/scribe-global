Even the year of her birth remains disputed—some sources say 1930, others 1939. Born into a family with roots near the Sundarbans, she developed a love for her homeland from early childhood. Her father Syed Ahmed's career took the family to Kolkata, where she grew up, matriculated from Loreto School, studied dance under Sadhona Bose, and sang—the seeds of artistry were planted in her long before she recognized them herself. After Partition, her father was transferred first to Comilla and then to Chittagong. Her family hoped she would study law and settle into a conventional life. But her heart was already set on a different horizon entirely. In 1950, she left for London.

Back home, under the guidance of architect Mazharul Islam, she and fellow artist Hamidur Rahman began working together at the Public Library. It was here that the most fraught chapter of her story would begin.

## The Shaheed Minar: A Dream Betrayed

Against the backdrop of the 1952 Language Movement, and despite the government's lack of cooperation, construction of the Shaheed Minar finally began in 1956. The project was so politically sensitive that no design competition or tender was ever announced publicly. Instead, East Pakistan's Chief Minister, Ataur Rahman, personally handed the design responsibility to the master artist Zainul Abedin, who in turn privately commissioned sketches and models from his two proteges, Hamidur and Nabera. Jean Delahaye, the government's architectural advisor, oversaw the process.

The essential truth is this: the design of the Shaheed Minar was a joint creation by Nabera and Hamidur, drawing its inspiration from Nabera's own sculptures, titled **"Seated Woman"**—works centered on the bond between a mother figure and her bowed child, a theme that would become the monument's emotional core. The *Pakistan Observer* of the time credited them jointly, in no uncertain terms. And yet, for reasons never explained, Nabera's name quietly disappeared from the official record. What's more striking still is that Hamidur—the very man who had created the design alongside her—never once spoke out publicly against this injustice.

The monument the two of them had envisioned was far grander, far more alive than what eventually rose from the ground. Their plan called for semicircular pillars, standing like a mother and her martyred child upon a raised platform, etched with countless eye-shaped motifs in yellow and deep blue glass—so that sunlight, striking the glass, would scatter across the grounds in rainbow light. There was to be a railing inscribed with Bengali letters, a symbolic contrast between bloodied footprints and footprints in black, and beyond that, a museum, a library, long murals chronicling the struggle for language, and a fountain shaped like an eye. It was, in every sense, meant to be a living complex.

Then, in 1958, martial law brought everything to a halt. Delahaye himself altered the proportions of the pillars in the sketches Hamidur had submitted, and what was ultimately built bore only a faint resemblance to the original vision. A committee appointed under Governor Azam Khan's orders stripped the design down further still, and the hastily completed monument was finally inaugurated on the 21st of February, 1963—unveiled by Hasina Begum, mother of the language martyr Barkat. After independence, the monument would stand once more, carrying the spirit of Ekushey forward, if only for a single day each year. But Nabera never came back to see it. And in truth, no one ever tried very hard to bring her back.

## "Inner Gaze," and Exile Abroad

In 1960, her first solo exhibition of sculpture, **"Inner Gaze"** (*Monolok*), opened at the Public Library—a moment that marked not just a turning point in her own career, but in the history of sculpture across the subcontinent. Whether driven by fear of military rule or by the quiet resentment of having been denied her due, Nabera once again set her sights on France.

Before settling there, she held a solo exhibition in Bangkok, showing sculptures forged from bronze, steel, and sheet metal, alongside welded stainless-steel pieces made from the discarded weapons and equipment of the Vietnam War—a stark attempt to confront the horror of that conflict head-on. In Bangkok, she was accompanied by the photographer Grégoire de Brunhoff. Not long after, she made Paris her permanent home.

In the years that followed, Ahmed wrote about her work, and in 1997 her name was announced as a recipient of the Ekushey Padak—an honor she never returned to accept. Bangladesh's ambassador later confirmed she was still alive. It was the artist Shahabuddin and his wife, Ana Islam, who eventually managed, through sheer persistence, to reestablish contact with her. The only further trace of connection came when her passport expired, and she reached out to the embassy official Iqtiar Chowdhury to arrange a new one.

During her lifetime, remarkably little was written about her work, and no organized archive was ever compiled in Bangladesh for future scholars. What remains is a small handful of sources: *"Nabera Ahmed,"* a collection edited by Abul Hasnat; *"Nohonnote,"* a documentary produced by the Bengal Foundation; and *"Nabera,"* a book by Hasnat Abdul Hai. In 1998, a solo exhibition of her work was held at the Dhaka museum, and a gallery there was named in her honor. She never once attended.

## Resentment, or Self-Preservation?

Resentment is not the same as anger. It is a deeper kind of sorrow, one that doesn't protest through outcry but through distance, through silence, through simply never coming back. The story of the Shaheed Minar is the first, and perhaps the deepest, wound of that resentment—a woman who had been an equal partner in imagining the nation's most sacred monument, only to have her name quietly written out of its history. It's a striking thought: here was someone who, in a deeply conservative era, chose sculpture as her medium, defied convention to live freely, and lived entirely on her own terms. And what did that cost her? Recognition replaced by erasure. Respect replaced by neglect. And, because she was a woman, an extra burden placed on top of it all.

Perhaps it was the weight of carrying that injustice alone that led her to understand her art would never be allowed to fully flourish on this soil. So she left—not as a retreat, but as a form of resistance expressed not through words, but through silence. When people asked why she never returned, her only answer was "personal reasons." Nothing more.

The fog surrounding Nabera's life—the uncertainty over her birth year, the false rumors of her death, the sheer scarcity of documented fact—none of this happened by accident. A person who stops speaking her mother tongue, who keeps herself hidden from her own countrymen for decades, does not become a stranger by chance. The artist Lalarukh Selim has pointed to two reasons: the conservative social climate of the time, and the injustice her contemporaries in the art world showed toward her work. Ana Islam has said simply that Nabera stayed away for personal reasons—though what those reasons actually were, no one has ever been able to say with certainty.

## The Final Journey

From 1984 onward, her life partner was Grégoire de Brunhoff, who once said that in Nabera's sculpture, material and form mattered above all else, and that she felt a profound connection to the rhythms of nature itself. In 2014, an exhibition of her work was held at a studio in Paris, where a photograph of *"The Goat of Chantemesle"* was displayed—a goat balanced on three legs, its head slightly lowered, rendered with such presence it seemed to possess a personality of its own.

That long journey finally came to rest in the village of Auteuil, just outside Paris. On one side of her coffin lay a single yellow rose; on the other, her beloved sparrow. And resting on her chest was the brochure from one of her exhibitions—as if art itself had remained her closest companion, from the very beginning to the very end.

In the end, Nabera Ahmed's life reads like an unfinished manuscript—its pages torn, its lines faded, its final chapter written entirely in silence. The country that failed to recognize her never made her lose herself; if anything, distance let her live, and remain distant, entirely on her own terms. A history this tragic, between an artist and her art, is rare indeed.
